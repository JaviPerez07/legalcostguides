South Carolina Lawyer Costs in 2026: What to Expect Before You Hire

On This Page

South Carolina legal fees sit in a 2026 planning range of about $175 to $350 per hour for many consumer, family, criminal, estate, real estate, and small-business matters, with a midpoint close to $263. But the figure that decides your total bill is scope, not the hourly rate. Many of the disputes people worry about most — an unpaid invoice, a withheld security deposit, a small contract fight — never need that hourly rate at all, because South Carolina routes them through magistrate court, where a person can often appear without a lawyer.

That magistrate small-claims track, capped at $7,500, is the organizing idea of this guide: it is the cheapest door into the South Carolina court system, and knowing where it ends tells you when you actually need to hire. Above that line, your matter moves to the Court of Common Pleas or Family Court, where attorney time, retainers, and the state's equitable-apportionment property rules start to drive the cost. The sections below lay out hourly rates, the real magistrate and circuit filing fees, fee structures, and official South Carolina resources so you can budget before you hire.

Why South Carolina Pricing Varies

South Carolina has roughly 16,500 active attorneys per the South Carolina Bar, with Columbia, Charleston, and Greenville as primary markets. Cost of living runs about 6% below the national average. The Charleston port, BMW manufacturing in Spartanburg, and the state's growing automotive and aerospace sectors drive specialty work. South Carolina's distinctive personal-injury verdict environment (especially in some Lowcountry counties) creates premium rates for plaintiff-side firms.

The one variable cost is sheriff service of process, which is billed separately on top of the magistrate fee, so budget for it when you plan a small-claims filing.

Confirm the current charge with the South Carolina Judicial Branch for the specific magistrate, circuit, or family court where you will file.

South Carolina Magistrate (Small Claims) Court Costs

South Carolina does not run a standalone "small claims court." Instead, small claims are heard in magistrate court, the state's local trial court for minor civil and criminal matters. On the civil side, a magistrate can hear a case worth up to $7,500 (SC Code §22-3-10) — that ceiling is the single most useful number for a consumer deciding whether to hire a lawyer at all.

The headline cost is modest. The magistrate fee to issue the summons and enter judgment in a civil action is $45 (SC Code §8-21-1010(A)(6)). A separate sheriff service fee is charged to deliver the summons to the defendant, so the realistic out-of-pocket to start a case is the $45 filing plus service. Because magistrate procedure is designed to be navigated without counsel, many claimants under the $7,500 line never pay an attorney's hourly rate.

The track fits straightforward, well-documented disputes: an unpaid invoice, a withheld security deposit, damage to property, or a small contract or consumer claim. It is a poor fit when the claim exceeds $7,500, hinges on complex expert proof, or carries long-term consequences (a default judgment, for example, can affect credit and collections). In those situations the matter belongs in the Court of Common Pleas, and an hourly-billed lawyer becomes worth the cost. If you want to estimate the filing side before you file, the small claims filing fee calculator is a useful starting point.

Lawyer Fee Structures in South Carolina

Most South Carolina lawyers use one of four structures: hourly billing, flat fees, contingency fees, or a retainer that is billed down as work is performed. Hourly billing is common when the scope may change, such as contested divorce, Common Pleas litigation, business disputes, and criminal defense with multiple hearings. Flat fees fit repeatable tasks such as simple wills, uncontested filings, formation documents, and some traffic or misdemeanor matters.

A typical consumer retainer in South Carolina often starts around $1,750 to $5,260 for contested work, with higher deposits for trial-heavy cases, emergency motions, or specialized business litigation. Contingency fees are most common in plaintiff-side injury and some employment matters, where the lawyer is paid from a recovery instead of billing monthly.

South Carolina does not cap ordinary consumer contingency fees. Rule 1.5 of the Rules of Professional Conduct (Rule 407, SCACR) requires only that the fee be reasonable, which is why plaintiff-side injury work commonly runs 33% to 40%. Two limits matter, though: contingency fees are prohibited entirely in domestic-relations matters (divorce, custody, support) and in criminal cases. (The tiered 25%-to-5% percentages you may see in South Carolina law apply only to outside counsel the State itself hires, not to your fee agreement.) Before signing any contingency agreement, ask whether case expenses are deducted before or after the attorney percentage, and whether the percentage changes if a lawsuit must be filed.

If you later disagree with a bill, the South Carolina Supreme Court's Resolution of Fee Disputes Board (created under Rule 416, SCACR) resolves fee and cost disputes under $50,000, and its decision is binding.

Cost by Practice Area in South Carolina

Personal injury: Most South Carolina injury firms work on contingency, typically 33% to 40% of the recovery, with no statewide cap on the percentage. You may owe no hourly fee, but medical-record charges, expert costs, deposition transcripts, and litigation expenses still come out of the net result. The state's plaintiff-friendly verdict environment in some Lowcountry counties pushes serious cases to require tens of thousands of dollars in case investment before settlement.

Divorce and family law: Divorce is filed in Family Court for a $150 fee. An uncontested divorce may be a flat fee on top of that when both spouses agree on property, support, and parenting; a contested one needs a retainer, with practical budgets from $2,625 to $15,750 or more once custody evaluations or business valuation enter. Because South Carolina apportions marital property equitably (fairly, not automatically 50/50, SC Code §20-3-620), the dispute over what counts as marital is often the real cost driver — and remember that contingency fees are not allowed in any family matter.

Criminal defense: A misdemeanor may quote from a few thousand dollars to the low five figures depending on charge severity, record, and trial posture; felony defense costs far more because investigation, motions, expert review, and trial preparation expand the work. Ask whether the quoted fee covers trial or only a pretrial resolution.

Business: Charleston port logistics, Spartanburg manufacturing, and the state's automotive and aerospace growth keep formation and contract work busy. Entity setup can be a flat-fee package, while contract drafting, partner disputes, and commercial litigation usually bill hourly and move quickly into a five-figure budget.

Estate planning: A simple will package is often the lowest-cost attorney service in the estate category. Revocable trusts, tax-sensitive planning, blended-family provisions, special-needs planning, or deed transfers raise the cost because the lawyer must coordinate documents and asset ownership.

Ways to Reduce Legal Costs in South Carolina

Start organized: bring a timeline, key documents, deadlines, names, and a one-page summary to the first consultation so the lawyer spends time on strategy and risk, not reconstruction. Ask for a staged plan — consultation, document review, first filing, negotiation, and litigation only if it becomes necessary. For a low-dollar dispute, also check whether it fits the $7,500 magistrate small-claims ceiling before paying for full representation.

For free or lower-cost help, check South Carolina Legal Services, the South Carolina Bar referral service, magistrate-court and courthouse self-help resources, and law school clinics where available. Limited-scope representation can also cut cost when you only need document review, coaching before a hearing, or help with a single filing. If you disagree with a bill, the South Carolina Bar's Resolution of Fee Disputes Board can resolve fee and cost disputes under $50,000, with a binding decision.

Mediation and ADR also lower spend when the dispute is mostly about money, parenting logistics, or contract terms rather than immediate safety or high-risk rights. Use the mediation cost guide to compare ADR pricing against a litigation budget.

A fourth route is to keep routine paperwork off the attorney's clock entirely. Documents like a state-specific will, a power of attorney, or a residential lease are drafted to your state's rules and are often the lowest-cost line in any legal budget. They are not a substitute for advice once a matter is contested, and nothing here applies to litigation.
